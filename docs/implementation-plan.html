Acute Encephalopathy App

An iPhone tool that walks a clinician through five bedside questions and returns a likelihood, the name of what they are seeing, next steps, and a sentence for the chart.

1The problem

Two groups of clinicians have been describing the same brain problem with different words. ICU doctors and geriatricians say delirium. Neurologists and many hospital notes say encephalopathy. Their research barely refers to each other: in the 2020 paper's analysis, almost 99% of references stayed within one word's camp.

The 2020 consensus fixed the vocabulary: acute encephalopathy is the thing going wrong inside the brain, and subsyndromal delirium, delirium and coma are the three ways it shows up at the bedside. What the paper did not give is a simple way to apply that at the bedside.

Why does that matter? Labels steer care. Write "septic encephalopathy" and people may stop looking for other causes. Write "delirium" and a known care bundle switches on. Write "altered mental status" and nobody knows what was actually found. Why does this keep happening? Nothing at the bedside forces you to name both the presentation and the cause, so people write whichever word they learned.

The branches the app must handle

Not actually newThis is the patient's baseline, so it is a long-term problem such as dementia, not acute encephalopathy.
ComaToo unresponsive to test attention, so the delirium questions can't be asked.
DeliriumAll four core features are present.
Subsyndromal deliriumSome features are present, but not all of them.

2What the app does

It asks the five questions from our step process, in order, one per screen:

1 · BaselineIs this new or worse? Yes, not sure, or no.
2 · ArousalHow awake? Pick one level on the 10-level RASS scale.
3 · FeaturesInattention, reduced awareness, fluctuation, other thinking change. Plus: could dementia explain it?
4 · Red flagsOne-sided signs, seizures, fever with stiff neck, fall on blood thinners.
5 · CauseMedications, withdrawal, infection, metabolic, oxygen/CO₂, bladder/bowel/pain, stroke, or not yet known.
ResultLikelihood (low, moderate or high), the presentation name, red-flag actions, next steps and a copy-ready chart note.
Why is there no percentage? No study has turned these criteria into a validated probability. Showing "78%" would look scientific without being so. Low, moderate and high are honest about what the rules can tell you. When could that change? A percentage can be added later, but only after real patient data shows what each answer pattern predicts.

4How the answers flow

Think of it as a funnel with two shortcuts. Answers go in at the top, the rules sort them, and one result comes out.

5The rules, in plain English

The rules are checked from top to bottom, and the first one that matches decides the result.

If…PresentationLikelihood
The change is not new (baseline)Acute encephalopathy unlikelyLow
RASS −4 or −5 (no response to voice)ComaHigh
All 4 core features present, no dementia doubtDeliriumHigh
All 4 present, but dementia could explain itPossible delirium on top of dementiaModerate
1 to 3 core features presentSubsyndromal deliriumModerate
None presentNo delirium featuresLow

Then two adjustments: if the baseline answer was "not sure", the likelihood drops one level. The motor type comes from RASS: above 0 is hyperactive, below 0 is hypoactive, exactly 0 is mixed or undetermined.

6Tricky cases and how the app handles them

SituationWhat the app doesWhy
Baseline unknown (no family, no notes)Allows "Not sure", lowers likelihood one level, and says soThe rules need an acute change. Guessing "yes" would overcall.
Patient is RASS −3 (stuporous)Still asks the feature questions, counted as hypoactiveThe paper leaves this zone unclear. RASS −3 still responds to voice, so testing is possible.
Known dementiaA separate "could dementia explain this?" switchDementia alone can produce some features (DSM-5 criterion D).
ComaSkips features and goes straight to red flagsYou cannot test attention in someone who won't respond to voice.
Several causesAll of them go in the note, joined with "and"Most delirium has more than one cause.
"Not yet known" causeClears the other causes. The note says "[cause not yet identified]" and a search reminder appearsThe paper's key point: the diagnosis isn't finished until a cause is named.
Red flag plus low likelihoodThe red-flag warning still shows at the topA one-sided weakness needs a scan whatever the delirium score says.
Patient is sedatedReminder on step 2: "assess off sedation where you can"Sedation drugs can mimic coma or delirium.

7Design choices and the reasons for them

One question per screenFewer mistakes when you are tired or interrupted. Same pattern as iPhone Settings.
Deep teal for every actionCalm and clinical. Warm clay and amber are kept only for likelihood and warnings, so color always means something.
Words, not just colorEvery likelihood also says "Low", "Moderate" or "High", so color-blind users lose nothing.
Big tap areasEverything is at least 44 points tall, Apple's minimum, so it works with gloves on.
Main button at the bottomWithin reach of your thumb on a large iPhone.
Quiet motionScreens rise gently, ticks pop and buttons press in slightly. All of it turns off if the phone's Reduce Motion setting is on.
Strong contrastEvery text color passes the accessibility standard (WCAG AA, at least 4.5:1). The ratios are on the canvas's style-tokens board.
The note names both parts"…due to cause, manifesting as presentation". This matches the 2020 consensus and avoids "altered mental status".

8Code worth reviewing

You don't need to read code. Each snippet below has a plain-English line saying what it means, so you can check the medicine is right.

if (acute === 'no')            → Low,  "Acute encephalopathy unlikely"
else if (coma)                 → High, "Coma"
else if (featuresPresent === 4 && !dementia) → High, "Delirium"
else if (featuresPresent === 4 && dementia)  → Moderate, "Possible delirium on top of dementia"
else if (featuresPresent >= 1) → Moderate, "Subsyndromal delirium"
else                           → Low,  "No delirium features"

In plain English: this is the table in section 5. Checking order matters: "not new" is checked first, so a patient at baseline can never be labelled delirious.

var coma = rass !== null && rass <= -4;
if (screen === 'arousal' && coma) next = 'flags';   // skip the feature questions

In plain English: RASS −4 or −5 counts as coma, and the app jumps past the feature questions. If you think RASS −3 should also count as coma, this "−4" is the one number to change.

if (acute === 'unsure' && level > 0) { level -= 1; }

In plain English: if you are not sure the change is new, the app drops the likelihood one level. A "high" becomes "moderate" until someone confirms the baseline.

doc = 'Acute encephalopathy due to ' + causes + ', manifesting as ' + subtype + presentation + '.'
// e.g. "Acute encephalopathy due to infection, manifesting as hypoactive delirium."

In plain English: this builds the chart sentence from your answers. It always names both the cause and what you saw. That pairing is the whole point of the 2020 paper.

9Build phases

  1. DonePhase 1 · Clickable prototype. All screens, rules and the chart note work on the design canvas.
  2. Phase 2 · Clinical review. You and one or two colleagues (ICU, neurology, geriatrics) review the wording, the coma cut-off, the red flags and the cause list.
  3. Phase 3 · Test patients. Write a set of made-up patients with the answer you expect for each, covering every row in sections 5 and 6. Then confirm the app gives the same answer every time.
  4. Phase 4 · iPhone web app. Package it so it installs to the home screen and works offline. Nothing is stored or sent, so no patient data leaves the phone.
  5. Phase 5 · Pilot. Try it on your service, compare the app's labels with your own, and refine before sharing more widely.

10Impact and limits

What it changes

One shared vocabulary in notes across specialties. Fewer vague "altered mental status" entries. A reminder that the job isn't done until a cause is found. Clearer documentation for handoffs and coding.

What it does not do

It does not replace the 4AT or CAM-ICU tests themselves. It records what those tests showed. It does not give a validated probability, and it has not yet been tested on real patients. It is decision support, and the clinician's judgement comes first.
